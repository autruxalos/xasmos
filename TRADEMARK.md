## What this means in practice

**You may:**
- Fork, modify, and redistribute the code under BSD-3-Clause terms.
- Build your own distribution based on XASMOS and give it its own name.
- Say your distribution is "based on XASMOS" or "powered by the Wayward
  kernel" — factual, attributive statements are always fine.
- Keep the kernel's own identification (boot messages, `ver` output,
  copyright headers) referring to Wayward/XASMOS/Autruxalos as originally
  written, unmodified.

**You may not:**
- Call your own distribution or fork "XASMOS" (or something confusingly
  similar) without written permission — pick your own name for your
  distribution, the way "Ubuntu" doesn't call itself "Debian."
- Strip or alter the existing copyright/authorship notices in the kernel,
  bootloader, or shell to imply you originated them.
- Use the XASMOS name, logo, or component names in a way that suggests
  your distribution is official, endorsed by, or affiliated with the
  original project, when it isn't.

## Why a separate trademark policy instead of a restrictive license

A permissive code license (BSD) and a trademark policy solve different
problems. The license controls what you can do with the *code*. The
trademark controls what you can do with the *name*. Keeping them separate
means companies and distros get real, unencumbered permissive rights to
the code (what makes BSD attractive to them in the first place), while the
project's identity and attribution stay intact regardless of how many
forks or distributions exist. This is the same approach used by Mozilla
(Firefox), Rust, and the Linux kernel itself.
